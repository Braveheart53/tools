Monitoring arithmetic

Sizing the monitoring stack, and getting the queries right

The install pages in this set get Prometheus, the exporters and Grafana running. Four questions come up immediately afterwards and none of them are answered there: how much disk the retention window will cost, what the scrape configuration actually ingests, why a panel intermittently goes blank, and whether a given metric needs differencing before it means anything.

This page holds those four relations in one place rather than repeating them on each of the four monitoring pages. Every equation is tagged Stated or Derived here and carries an information button that opens a variable table with units.

1. Disk cost of a retention window

The Prometheus storage documentation gives the sizing relation directly, and two figures that make it usable: the server "stores an average of only 1-2 bytes per sample", and a size-based retention limit should be set "to, at most, 80-85% of your allocated Prometheus disk space"[18].

Prometheus on-disk size

Stated
\[ D \;=\; T_{\text{ret}} \cdot \lambda \cdot \beta \]

This is the sizing relation given in the Prometheus storage documentation, which also states that the server averages one to two bytes per stored sample and recommends setting a size-based retention limit to at most 80–85 % of the allocated disk[18]. Reducing the number of series is more effective than reducing the sample rate, because samples within a series compress well and each new series does not.

FlagDocumented defaultNote

Reduce series before reducing sample rate. The documentation states that reducing the number of series is "likely more effective, due to compression of samples within a series"[18]. The practical reading: lengthening the scrape interval loses resolution for a modest saving, while dropping a high-cardinality metric saves far more and loses nothing you were looking at. Per-process and per-disk-partition metrics are the usual offenders on a Windows host.

2. What the scrape configuration ingests

The storage relation needs \( \lambda \), which is not published anywhere because it depends entirely on your own prometheus.yml. It follows from the job definitions.

Ingested sample rate from the scrape configuration

Derived here
\[ \lambda \;=\; \sum_{j} \frac{n_{j}}{T_{s,j}} \]

Feeds the storage relation above. Worth computing before adding an exporter rather than after, because a GPU exporter publishing a few hundred series per card across four nodes is a larger step than it looks.

  1. Each scrape job \( j \) collects \( n_{j} \) distinct time series from its targets on every scrape.
  2. It scrapes once every \( T_{s,j} \) seconds, so it contributes \( n_{j}/T_{s,j} \) samples per second.
  3. Jobs are independent, so the total is the sum over jobs.
  4. Worked example for the four-node stack on this page set. windows_exporter publishing roughly 1500 series on each of 4 nodes at \( T_{s} = 15\ \mathrm{s} \) gives \( 6000/15 = 400\ \mathrm{sample\,s^{-1}} \). An nvidia_gpu_exporter adding about 120 series on each of 3 GPU nodes at 15 s gives \( 360/15 = 24 \). llama-server --metrics adding roughly 30 series at 15 s gives 2. Total \( \lambda \approx 426\ \mathrm{sample\,s^{-1}} \).
  5. Substituting into the storage relation with \( T_{\text{ret}} = 15\ \mathrm{d} = 1.296\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{s} \) and \( \beta = 2\ \mathrm{B} \): \( D = 1.296\times10^{6} \times 426 \times 2 = 1.10\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{B} \approx 1.1\ \mathrm{GB} \). Allow several times that for the write-ahead log, memory-mapped chunks and compaction headroom.
  6. The series counts above are order-of-magnitude figures for planning, not measurements. Replace them with prometheus_tsdb_head_series read from your own server once it has been running.

3. Why a panel goes blank, and the window that fixes it

A dashboard panel that works most of the time and empties occasionally is almost never a broken exporter. It is a range-vector window too close to the scrape interval.

Minimum range-vector window for rate()

Derived here
\[ \left\lfloor \frac{w}{T_{s}} \right\rfloor \;\ge\; 2 + f \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad w \;\ge\; (2+f)\,T_{s} \]

The usual advice to make the window four times the scrape interval is this inequality with \( f = 2 \). Panels that go blank intermittently are nearly always a window too close to the scrape interval, not a broken exporter.

  1. rate() is documented as the per-second average rate of increase across the range vector, computed between its first and last samples and extrapolated to the ends of the range to allow for missed scrapes[19]. It therefore needs two samples inside the window to return anything. The PromQL function reference states that two-float-sample minimum explicitly only for deriv and predict_linear; for rate it is the observed behaviour rather than a documented guarantee, so treat this step as empirical.
  2. A window of width \( w \) at scrape interval \( T_{s} \) contains \( \lfloor w/T_{s} \rfloor \) samples when every scrape succeeds.
  3. Allowing \( f \) consecutive scrapes to fail or arrive late without the panel emptying requires \( 2+f \) slots rather than 2.
  4. Rearranging gives \( w \ge (2+f)T_{s} \).
  5. For the \( T_{s} = 15\ \mathrm{s} \) jobs on this page set, tolerating two missed scrapes gives \( w \ge 60\ \mathrm{s} \), which is why the dashboard expressions use [1m].
  6. Raising \( T_{s} \) without raising \( w \) is the usual regression. Pin the relationship by templating the window from the scrape interval instead of hard-coding it.

The regression to guard against. Raising scrape_interval without raising every window that depends on it breaks panels that were previously fine, and it breaks them intermittently, which makes it hard to attribute. In Grafana, template the window from a dashboard variable rather than hard-coding [1m] in twenty expressions, so one edit moves all of them.

Two further documented properties are worth knowing. rate() extrapolates "to the ends of the time range, allowing for missed scrapes", and when combining it with an aggregation you should "always take a rate() first, then aggregate"[19]. Aggregating first destroys the per-series counter-reset handling and silently produces a wrong number rather than an error.

4. Counters and gauges are not interchangeable

The GPU exporters publish both kinds of metric, and applying the wrong treatment produces a plausible-looking panel rather than an obvious failure. That makes it the most expensive class of mistake in a monitoring setup.

Utilisation from a cumulative busy-time counter

Derived here
\[ U(w) \;=\; \frac{c(t) - c(t-w)}{w} \;=\; \mathrm{rate}\bigl(c[w]\bigr), \qquad 0 \le U \le n_{\text{dev}} \]

Worth stating explicitly because the GPU exporters publish both kinds of metric and they are not interchangeable: a cumulative busy-seconds counter must be differenced, while an instantaneous percentage gauge must not be. Applying rate() to a gauge, or omitting it on a counter, are the two failure modes that produce plausible-looking but wrong dashboards.

  1. A busy-time counter \( c(t) \) accumulates seconds during which the device was executing work, and only ever increases.
  2. Over a window \( w \) the device was busy for \( c(t) - c(t-w) \) seconds out of \( w \) seconds elapsed.
  3. The ratio of those two is the fraction of wall-clock time the device was busy, which is the definition of utilisation.
  4. That ratio is precisely what PromQL rate() computes over a counter. The function reference states that breaks in monotonicity, such as a counter reset when the exporter or driver restarts, are adjusted for automatically[19], which is why the raw difference must not be taken by hand.
  5. When the counter aggregates \( n_{\text{dev}} \) devices the result saturates at \( n_{\text{dev}} \) rather than 1, so divide by the device count before plotting a percentage.
  6. \( w \) must satisfy the window condition derived above, otherwise the panel empties whenever a scrape is missed.

Gauge, instantaneous GPU utilisation per cent, temperature, VRAM in use, fan speed Plot it directly Wrapped in rate() it returns a near-zero line, or nothing, because a gauge is not monotonic

How to tell which you have. Open the exporter's own metrics endpoint and read the # TYPE line, which names the kind for every metric:

# The documented check for the NVIDIA exporter, which the set's existing
# pages install at port 9835.
Invoke-WebRequest http://localhost:9835/metrics |
  Select-Object -ExpandProperty Content |
  Select-String -Pattern '^# TYPE'

Do this once per exporter and write the kinds down. It takes a minute and it removes the whole class of mistake above.
